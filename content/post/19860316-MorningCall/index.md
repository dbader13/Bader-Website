Muhlenberg College changed the rules for its ninth-annual high school mathematics contest to make it a balance between speed and reasoning.

The team from Liberty High School – which triumphed easily the previous eight years when speed alone was the key factor – won anyway. But it had to struggle to do it.

Salisbury High School came in a close second, thanks to its top-placing in the reasoning section. Salisbury’s team captain was Bradley Mann, a 16-year- old sophomore who has exhausted the math offerings at his school and is in his fourth math course at Muhlenberg.

The scores in percentages were 56 for Liberty, 48 for Salisbury, a tie at 40 for Emmaus and Freedom, 34 for Parkland and 18 for Allen.

William Brewster, 17, a senior, headed the winning Liberty team. He is a son of James and Frances Faile of Bethlehem. The other team members were Neil Szanyi and **David Bader**, both juniors.

For the ninth consecutive year, the team was coached by teacher Doris Helms, who bypassed any comments about whether the new rules were aimed specifically at Liberty.

She said, however, it was nice that every team got 40 seconds to answer the questions that relied somewhat on speed.

In past years, a question would be placed on a screen before a group of three or four teams – up to three members per team. The first team to press its buzzer got to answer – 10 points for being correct and minus five for incorrect.

The other teams were locked out.

It was possible for a team to compete and never get a chance to answer a question.

Liberty clobbered one and all with that system.

This year, there was the same pattern of putting questions on the screen. For example, with the question, “Find the smallest positive integer such that its cube is greater than 200 times itself,” each team had 40 seconds to write out its answer and hold it up for the judge. A correct answer was 10 points and an incorrect minus 8 points.

All the teams had a chance to answer with that system.

For the other half of the test, each team was given six mathematical theorems or exercises and the reasoning that went with them. Each was meant to sound plausible. But each contained fallacies.

The task for the team was to analyze the reasoning, find out what was wrong, explain what should be correct and write it all out on paper. An hour was given for this.

Robert Stump of Muhlenberg’s math department told the students, “We’ve never tried this before. A number of people try to play games with mathematics. They divide by zero and prove that one equals two.”

This exercise was to be something like that, he said.

Brewster, the Liberty captain, said the new system spread the points around somewhat. “It was just a speed contest in the past, and Miss Helms trains us for speed,” he said. “This year, speed was not such an important factor.

“We were a little bit uncomfortable coming in because we won eight times in a row.”

In the reasoning section, Brewster said the Liberty team divided up the labor. But amid smiles, he said he and teammate Bader spent 20 minutes arguing about one geometry problem before they resolved the issue.

Mann, the Salisbury captain, has been a member of the Salisbury team since eighth grade. He had only one teammate this year, Alex Bollendorf, also a sophomore.

“We couldn’t get a third person to go with us,” Mann said. And the listed coach, Richard Gross, did not attend.

Mann said he liked the new system. “With previous years, Liberty did it on speed alone,” he said. “With the written part this year, I felt better with the time period to work it out.”

Freedom captain Charrissa Lin, 16, who is headed for the Massachusetts Institute of Technology next year, said of the reasoning portion, “Did you hear us yelling in there? We were screaming and arguing. If we’d been in there longer, we’d have pulled each other’s hair out.

“It was interesting and really cool – and also frustrating.”

The Muhlenberg math department and the school’s math club sponsored the competition.

Freedom coach Nancy Eckert said the event takes extensive work on the part of the college.

Finally, Stump’s answer sheet listed 15 as the smallest positive integer such that its cube is greater than 200 times itself.
