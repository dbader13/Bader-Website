Testimony of David A. Bader, Ph.D.  
Distinguished Professor of Data Science  
Director, Institute for Data Science  
Associate Dean for Research, Ying Wu College of Computing  
New Jersey Institute of Technology

Before the New Jersey General Assembly Science, Innovation and Technology Committee on October 19, 2026

*Artificial Intelligence and Society*

## 1. Opening ##

Chairman Tully, Vice Chairman Singh, and members of the committee, thank you for inviting me to participate on behalf of the New Jersey Institute of Technology.

I am David Bader, Distinguished Professor of Data Science at NJIT in Newark, where I direct the Institute for Data Science and serve as Associate Dean for Research in the Ying Wu College of Computing. 

You will hear today from researchers who study what AI models can do. My vantage point is different. I have spent nearly thirty years building the machines underneath AI: the processors, networks, power, and software that make large-scale computing work. In 1998 I built the first Linux supercomputer from off-the-shelf parts, and that architecture now sits under essentially every cloud region and AI data center running today. I also served on Governor Murphy’s AI Task Force with three NJIT colleagues, and in the interest of full disclosure, I co-founded Adapter, an AI infrastructure startup.

You asked about developments in AI, its use across society and the economy, and the concerns it raises. Our view is that AI is already here, it runs on physical infrastructure this state can shape, and most of the harms worth worrying about are specific enough to fix. We do take the near-term risks seriously. What I don’t accept is that the answer is to stop.

## 2. Current State ##

That history shapes how I see AI today. In 1998, the hard part of supercomputing was getting a machine at all. Today, the hard part is plugging it in. AI is physical. Every answer from a chatbot draws power from the same grid that serves your constituents, and real-time AI strains that grid more than steady batch work. The binding constraint on frontier AI right now is electricity, transformers, substations, interconnect queues, and trained staff. We are already power limited.

That makes the buildout a long-term bet. We estimate a GPU trains AI models for about two years, then answers queries for roughly five more. And the substations and transmission lines we build to feed it will be here for decades. So, the better answer is often smarter software running on the hardware we already have.

AI has also changed from something that answers questions into something that acts. These systems write and run code and carry out multistep tasks with access to email, files, and accounts. I use one. Every Monday it scans grants.gov and drafts funding alerts for NJIT’s 66 computing faculty, and nothing goes out until I review it. With these agents, the risk is often the connection, not the model.
And people are adopting AI faster than we are building ways to check it. Generative AI reached 53 percent of the population within three years, faster than the PC or the internet, while documented AI incidents rose from 233 in 2024 to a record 362 last year. Nearly every leading developer reports how well its models perform, but the reporting on safety is spotty.

## 3. AI in New Jersey and Beyond ##

New Jersey is out in front. A Rutgers survey last year found that 74 percent of residents had used an AI tool, versus 68 percent nationally. Forty-one percent of working New Jerseyans use it on the job every week. Forty-five percent of students use AI often for schoolwork. State government uses it too, with more than 7,000 state workers on the NJ AI Assistant by late 2024. And 57 percent of residents expect AI to eliminate more jobs than it creates.

Access to AI changes what people are capable of. Two New Jersey high school students recently worked with my group at NJIT on Princeton’s FlyWire challenges, mapping the wiring of a fruit fly’s brain, and helped us take first place in one challenge and second in another. The AI turned what would typically be weeks of literature review into minutes. High schoolers competed at the frontier of neuroscience and won.

Other governments around the world are learning how to deal with potential costs of AI as well. The European Union, usually the strictest regulator, has delayed its rules for high-risk uses such as hiring until December 2027, while its requirement that AI-generated content be marked takes effect December 2 of this year. The EU’s emphasis is shifting to specific, provable harms.[MB1.1]

## 4. Concerns ##

It is the specific, provable harms that concern us most. Deepfake voice and video are being used for fraud and election manipulation. Spear phishing runs at industrial scale, automated tools probe the networks of utilities, hospitals, and schools, and model-written code goes into production without review. Researchers have documented hallucination and bias in systems deciding loans, hiring, and medical triage. Data pipelines go unaudited, and brittle automation is quietly trusted in power, water, and transportation.

New Jersey is already responding. Since April 2025, using a deceptive deepfake to commit a crime has been a third-degree offense, and 79 percent of residents polled by Rutgers say creating AI images of real people without permission should be illegal.

These are engineering, procurement, and liability problems. They are solvable, and they are not our extinction. My test for a real risk is whether you can name the mechanism, the failure path, and the mitigation. An aerospace engineer can tell you exactly how a turbine fails and what the inspection interval is. Most extinction talk fails that test; it is a mood, not a mechanism. A model is software on hardware with an operator, a power feed, and an off switch.

## 5. Legislative Relevance ##

The four bills on today’s agenda, sponsored by Chairman Tully and Assemblyman Macurdy, address harms of exactly this kind. We are not here to take a position on them. From an engineer’s view, rules like these work when a qualified party can test compliance and it is clear who is responsible when a system fails. With that in mind, we have four requests.

First, protect people from the harms we can already prove. If someone misuses your name, your face, or your voice, you should have a remedy. People should be able to tell where an image or a recording came from, and 85 percent of New Jersey residents say they want to know when they’re dealing with AI. Platforms should answer for harmful generative AI, starting with the exploitation of children. But we would not build enforcement on detection alone. I don’t know of a watermark or a detector that can’t be beaten.

Second, require real evaluation instead of vendor self-assessment. It matters most when AI is deciding someone’s loan, job, or health care. As I’ve said before, if the only people able to evaluate frontier models are the people selling them, we get theology instead of engineering. A disclosure to the Attorney General is only as good as someone’s ability to check it.

Third, set security standards for AI in critical infrastructure and in every system the state buys. Give each system the narrowest permission that does the job, keep a human in the loop for anything that cannot be undone, log what it does, and take away access nobody is using or needs.

Fourth, invest in the capacity underneath all of this. We’re already power limited, so plan generation, transmission, and efficiency alongside data center growth. Build public computing so universities can test these systems on their own. Right now the best ideas in AI are held back less by talent than by access to computing. New Jersey has the talent. Let’s carry forward the Task Force’s recommendations on AI literacy and workforce training. Banning AI would simply push usage underground.

For the same reason, we cannot support a pause or moratorium. It would shift the work to places with cheaper power and fewer rules, and that would be worse for safety, not better.

Every one of these requests depends on people who can do the work. Someone has to test these systems independently, train the workforce that will deploy and secure them, and run the computing that makes any of it possible. The New Jersey Institute of Technology is already leading on these and many other AI-related topics. More than a thousand of our students are in AI-related degree programs, including a new bachelor’s degree in Enterprise AI built around deploying and securing these systems responsibly, and we offer free AI micro-credentials to every student, faculty and staff member. Our researchers study the bias, security, and infrastructure questions I have described, our AI Exploration Day brought New Jersey educators and policymakers to campus this past spring, and we are a partner in the state’s agreement with NVIDIA to bring serious research computing to New Jersey. We welcome the chance to continue to work with this committee on these and other AI-related issues.

## 6. Close ##

I built that first Linux supercomputer so scientists could get time on a machine they could never afford alone. The companies may own much of today’s infrastructure, but how people use these tools is still in our hands. Put serious computing in the hands of New Jersey’s researchers and students, and they will find uses for it that nobody planned but that we will all benefit from.

Thank you again, Chairman Tully, Vice Chairman Singh, and members of the committee. I am happy to take your questions.

Contact: Matt Bonasia, Chief of State Government Affairs, New Jersey Institute of Technology
